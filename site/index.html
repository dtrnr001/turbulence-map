How to read this map

What the dots are

Each dot is a pilot report (a "PIREP"): a pilot telling air traffic control what the ride was like at that spot and altitude. They're real observations, not forecasts, and most are from the last hour or two. Tap a dot for details.

What the colors mean

  • Light: slight bumps. You notice the seatbelt; drinks ripple.
  • Moderate: real bumps. Drinks spill, walking is hard, and the seatbelt sign is on.
  • Severe: hard jolts. Loose items fly around. This is rare and gets an urgent report.
  • Extreme: violent and very rare.

"In-between" colors mean the pilot reported a range, like light to moderate.

Chop vs. turbulence

Chop is quick, rhythmic bumpiness without much change in altitude, like a boat on small waves. Turbulence means the plane is actually pushed up, down or sideways. Clear-air turbulence happens away from clouds, often near the jet stream.

Occasional, intermittent, continuous

How often it happened: occasional is less than a third of the time, intermittent is a third to two thirds, and continuous is most of the time.

The aircraft matters

The same air feels much rougher in a small plane than in an airliner. Each report says what kind of aircraft reported it, and you can filter by aircraft size.

My flight

Type your airports in order, including layovers (for example JFK ORD DEN), or your flight numbers (for example DL1234 DL567), then tap Show. The map draws the most direct path between each pair of airports and highlights the reports, hazard forecasts and severe weather warnings within the distance you choose. Airports anywhere in the world work, but pilot reports and G-AIRMETs cover mainly the US, so over oceans and abroad the severe weather warnings are the main guide. Real flights often stray from the direct path to avoid weather or follow airways, so treat it as a guide. Flight-number lookups use a free community database, so check that the airports match your booking.

Trip timeline

Once a route is set, a bar along the bottom shows the whole trip from left to right, drawn to scale: each flight, each layover (with its airport code), and turbulence icons where bumpy reports and turbulence forecasts and warnings sit along the way. Flight times are estimates (about 480 mph plus 35 minutes per flight for taxi, climb and descent), and you can choose how long to allow per layover in My flight. Reports describe the air right now, so conditions will change by the time you get there. Tap an icon for details, or tap the title to fold the bar away. Turn on Fun tips for a lighthearted guess at a smooth stretch to use the bathroom, and a heads-up to finish your drink before the bumps.

Overlays

Hazard forecasts (US) (dashed shapes) show where moderate turbulence, icing, low clouds and strong winds are expected over the US. Pilots call these G-AIRMETs. Severe weather warnings (solid shapes) cover more serious hazards: severe turbulence, thunderstorms, icing and volcanic ash, worldwide, including over oceans. Pilots call these SIGMETs.

Radar and winds aloft

The checkboxes at the bottom turn each layer on or off. Press and hold one to see its color key (one at a time, on the left); tap the map to close it. Besides the pilot reports, hazard forecasts and severe weather warnings, you can add radar (rain and storms from NOAA's quality-controlled radar mosaic, which filters out birds, bugs and ground clutter; refreshed every 5 minutes) and winds aloft (forecast jet-stream winds around 34,000 ft, where airliners cruise). Turbulence is more common near storms and along the edges of a strong jet stream, so these help explain the reports.

Where the data comes from

NOAA's Aviation Weather Center; radar from NOAA's MRMS mosaic; winds from the GFS model via Open-Meteo (CC BY 4.0). The map refreshes itself every few minutes. The pill at the top says how old the newest data is (green is fresh, amber is getting old, red means the feed has stalled), and you can tap it to refresh right away. It's for curiosity and context, not flight planning.
